Translating dense quantitative results into figures that non-specialists can read and act on is one of the things I do best. Examples below are from my published research and dissertation work. All figures designed and produced by me; tooling noted under each.

<img src='/assets/images/datavis_dissertation_brainschematic.png'>

*Tools: Adobe Illustrator · MATLAB*

Schematic diagram pairing anatomy with hierarchical findings: songbird brains contain two interacting systems for vocal communication: one for production (*a*), one for perception (*b-c*). My dissertation work showed that the elements of a bird's song (syllables) are learned from experience, while their sequential ordering (syntax) is shaped by species genetics. The diagrams highlight the physical closeness of the vocal-motor and auditory systems, and the importance of auditory processing circuits for behavioral function.

**Edwards JA**. **2025**. [Play with the changes: Innate rules for learned vocal communication in songbirds](https://academiccommons.columbia.edu/doi/10.7916/7bt6-xb79). *PhD Dissertation*. Columbia University.

<img width=600 src='/assets/images/datavis_SciRep.png'>

*Tools: MATLAB · custom feature pipelines · spectrogram visualization · Adobe Illustrator*

Multi-panel figure showing how an NLP-style sequence model generates and evaluates predictions on acoustic data. *Top panels (a-b):* color grids showing how each syllable's acoustic features are matched against species-typical patterns to generate a predicted sequence. *Bottom panels (c):* spectrograms comparing the model's prediction against what the bird actually sang, with a quantitative match score. On average, the model predicted song sequences as accurately as the bird's own tutor did — suggesting syntax is shaped by species identity rather than experience. Like large language models that learn the statistical rules of human language, this model learns the statistical rules of a species' songs. The approach generalizes to detecting and comparing sequential structure in any complex audio signal, including speech.

**Edwards JA**, Woolley SMN. **2026**. [A species rules syntax model accurately organizes birdsong syllables into songs](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-44602-5). *Scientific Reports*. In press (*available online*).

<img src='/assets/images/datavis_JNeuro.png'>

*Tools: MATLAB · custom metric design · spectrogram visualization · Adobe Illustrator*

Causal-inference study visualized as a side-by-side comparison; exemplar case (*a-b*) leading into population results (*c-d*) in one figure. By cross-fostering birds with tutors of a different species — a controlled experimental manipulation — I could isolate which features of song are genetically determined vs. learned from experience. The figure compares the songs of cross-tutored birds against their tutors' song and against their own species' songs across 9 temporal and sequence-level features using a custom-designed "bias" metric. Result: cross-tutored birds learned foreign syllables but arranged them in sequences typical of their own species — sequences they'd never heard. Syllables are shaped by experience; syntax is shaped by species genetics.

**Edwards JA**, Rivera M, Woolley SMN. **2025**. [The temporal organization of learned vocal behavior is predicted by species rather than experience](https://www.jneurosci.org/content/45/11/e0576242025). *Journal of Neuroscience* 45: e0576242025

<img src='/assets/images/datavis_dissertation_ephys.png'>

*Tools: Python · ImageJ · MATLAB · custom raster + spectrogram pipeline · Adobe Illustrator*

Multi-species, multi-neuron comparison combining experimental methods, stimulus, and neural responses in one figure. *Top (a-b):* schematic and histological reconstruction of recording electrode penetrations. *Middle (c):* spectrograms of song stimuli, with syllables color-coded by species. *Bottom (c):* raster plots where each tick is a single neuron firing and each row is one trial of the same stimulus. The figure validates that the brain areas recorded from contain neurons selective for songs of their own species over other species' songs. The major finding of the project was that the acoustics of individual syllables are encoded by individual neurons (the "what") while species-specific song timing is encoded by interactions of neurons in circuits (the "when"). The hierarchical encoding pattern mirrors the hierarchical structure of the behavior itself.

**Edwards JA**. **2025**. [Play with the changes: Innate rules for learned vocal communication in songbirds](https://academiccommons.columbia.edu/doi/10.7916/7bt6-xb79). *PhD Dissertation*. Columbia University.
