Three interactive tools for exploring data that doesn't fit in a spreadsheet: high-dimensional, time-evolving, messy and unlabeled. Each was built to let researchers (or stakeholders, or me) ask questions of complex data without writing code. I built them for neuroscience and animal behavior data; the tooling problems generalize.

---

## Ephys Explorer
*Real-time 3D visualization of high-dimensional time-series data*

A custom interactive tool for exploring how thousands of points evolve in space and time. Built so collaborators could see structure in their data without writing a line of code.

The animation below shows **1,700 neurons in a living brain activating as a bird hears one of its own species' songs**. Each point is a neuron; its size shows its firing rate. The two clouds are the left and right auditory cortex. The frequency spectrum of the sound scrolls below in real time, so you can watch the brain tracking the structure of the song as it unfolds, slowed 100× so the dynamics are visible.

The interface revealed spatial patterns, outliers, and stimulus-response structure that would be invisible in a table or a standard 2D plot. Adopted by additional research teams as their default exploratory tool for large multi-electrode datasets.

## Ephys Decoder
*Population decoding and latent-space analysis for time-series signals*

What does a brain actually hear? This tool **reconstructs the audio signal that a bird's auditory cortex encoded while listening to a song** — taking the activity of a population of neurons and decoding it back to the sound that produced it.

Below: a 5-second song stimulus, followed by the same song reconstructed entirely from neural activity. The two should sound surprisingly similar.

| [🔊 stimulus audio](/assets/docs/stim_real_proc.wav) | [🔊 reconstruction audio](/assets/docs/stim_recon_proc.wav) |

The tool also implements latent-space analysis (PCA over neural population activity) to visualize the geometry of how the brain represents sound — useful for spotting structure that classification metrics alone don't capture.

## SylLabeler
*Annotation pipeline turning raw recordings into labeled sequence data for ML*

Sequence modeling needs discrete labeled units, the same way NLP needs words before it can model sentences. For animal vocalizations, those units don't arrive pre-segmented. They have to be identified, classified, and tracked across tens of thousands of events and hundreds of subjects. SylLabeler replaced a fragmented manual workflow with a **single interface handling segmentation, labeling, dimensionality reduction, and cluster-based classification in one place.**

Cut per-project labeling time ~80% across three teams. One dataset it produced — 30k+ labeled acoustic events from 180 subjects — became the training data for the ["species rules" syntax model](/2026/01/01/sci-viz) published in *Scientific Reports* (2026).
